# Evidence Strategy: Selecting What to Use

Gathering evidence and choosing what to *lead* are different jobs. The
gathering phase is exhaustive; the selection phase is ruthless. These
principles were learned from counsel's review of a real trial book — most
of them by deleting things that had seemed helpful.

**A boundary before the strategy:** selection applies to what a party
*emphasizes in argument and leads in their own case*. It never applies to
disclosure obligations. Financial disclosure (FLA s. 5, PCFR Part 4, SCFR
Part 5) is mandatory, and concealing disclosable documents or lying about
the existence of evidence is misconduct that destroys cases and can be
punished. "Don't lead it" is strategy; "don't disclose it" is a different
thing the user must never do. If the user asks to hide something
disclosable, explain the duty and the consequences plainly.

## Start from the burden of proof

For every issue, establish who must prove what before deciding what
evidence to lead. The claimant must establish entitlement (to support, to
a division, to an order); the respondent does not have to disprove a case
that was never proven.

This reframes evidence selection for a responding party: the question is
not "what shows I'm right?" but "does this help the claimant meet a burden
they might otherwise fail?" In the source case, the claimant's own
financial statement showed expenses below income — the most effective
response was to lead *nothing* on the point and let her evidence speak.
Over-proving is a real failure mode: every extra exhibit is another thing
to be cross-examined on.

## Every exhibit cuts both ways

Before adding any piece of evidence, ask explicitly: **"What could the
other party do with this?"**

- Evidence of generosity ("I offered her money after she left") reads as
  ability to pay and acknowledgment of obligation.
- Evidence of a warm bond with a stepchild supports the claim that you
  stood in the place of a parent — exactly what a stepparent-support
  claimant must prove.
- Evidence of the other party's instability or vulnerability generates
  sympathy and supports their dependency claim.
- Detailed evidence of your own household spending proves the standard of
  living the other party will claim compensation for.

If an item helps both sides, weigh which side it helps more — and
remember that the side with the burden benefits from the record being
fuller. When in doubt, leave it out and keep it in reserve.

## Objective, third-party-facing acts are heavyweight evidence

How the parties presented themselves to institutions — employers,
insurers, the CRA, landlords, schools — counts for more than private
feelings, because it is documented, contemporaneous, and aimed at parties
with no stake in the litigation. Enrolling a partner as a "spouse" for
extended health benefits, filing taxes as common-law, listing someone as
an emergency contact or co-tenant: each is evidence of how the
relationship was held out. Inventory these early, on both sides of the
ledger — the user needs to know which institutional acts cut against them
before deciding which factors to contest.

## Keep to the relevant period

Establish what time period each legal test actually examines, and trim
evidence outside it. Whether a relationship was marriage-like is assessed
during cohabitation, not before it; conduct from years before the parties
lived together generally proves nothing about the relationship they lived.
Exceptions exist — pre-relationship *agreements* about how the
relationship would work (financial arrangements, expectations) can matter.
The discipline: for each exhibit, name the element of the legal test it
goes to; if it precedes or postdates the period that element examines, it
is probably clutter.

Income timing is a special case of this. Support analyses can use current
income, but entitlement is assessed against the relationship's standard of
living. If income changed sharply post-separation (new job, equity
vesting), make the timing unmissable in the financial evidence, or the
other side will happily blur it.

## Settlement communications stay out

Genuine attempts to settle — offers, mediation discussions,
"without prejudice" correspondence — are privileged and generally
inadmissible to prove liability. Showing the court "I tried to be
reasonable" is not a thing; that is what trials are for. Keep settlement
content out of affidavits, trial books, and exhibits. In the source case,
settlement material crept into trial documents and had to be found and
stripped late, under deadline. Screen for it at selection time: anything
marked "without prejudice," and any substantive offer to resolve, stays
in the correspondence folder and out of the evidence.

(Settlement offers can matter to *costs* in Supreme Court after judgment
— a different use, raised at the right moment. See `post-judgment.md`.)

## Sympathy is evidence too

Judges are human. Material that makes the other party pitiable — health
struggles, repeated job losses, their own self-critical words — can hurt
you even when it is logically helpful, by recasting the dispute as strong
vs. vulnerable. State the facts the legal test needs ("she was employed
when we met; she works now") without the catalogue of failures in
between. If a fact is both necessary and sympathetic to the other side,
lead it neutrally and briefly.

## Adverse facts: acknowledge, don't emphasize

Facts that cut against the user and are already in the record (or certain
to come out) should be acknowledged plainly and framed honestly — denial
or visible avoidance costs more than the fact itself. The skill's accuracy
standards (`evidence-standards.md`) are not suspended for bad facts. The
strategic choice is emphasis, never falsity.

## A selection checklist

For each candidate exhibit, in one pass:

1. Which issue and which element of the legal test does it go to?
2. Who has the burden on that element?
3. What does it give the other side?
4. Is it within the legally relevant time period?
5. Is it settlement-privileged?
6. Does it generate sympathy, and for whom?
7. Is it already proven by something safer?

If the user's lawyer (full-scope or unbundled) reviews the evidence,
treat their selections as controlling — record their guidance in the
case project's `strategy/` folder and consult it before every revision
of trial materials.
