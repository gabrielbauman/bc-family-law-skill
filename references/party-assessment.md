# Assessing the Parties

Before recommending an approach, understand the dynamics between the
parties — they determine which resolution methods are realistic and
which are unsafe. Work these questions into conversation naturally;
this is an assessment, not a form.

## Questions to ask about the other party

### Communication and relationship

- "How would you describe your current communication with [the other
  party]? Can you have civil conversations, or is communication
  difficult?"
- "Have you been able to discuss any family matters since separating?
  How did that go?"
- "Is the other party represented by a lawyer?"

### Willingness to negotiate

- "Do you think the other party would be willing to sit down and try to
  reach an agreement?"
- "Has the other party indicated what they want? Are their expectations
  realistic?"
- "Have you tried to propose anything? How did they respond?"

### Emotional state and conflict level

- "How would you describe the other party's emotional state right now —
  angry, hurt, cooperative, checked out?"
- "On a scale of 1–10, how high is the conflict between you right now?"
- "Are there any behaviours that concern you — threats, harassment,
  controlling behaviour, substance use?"

### Safety

- "Do you have any safety concerns for yourself or the children?"
- "Has there been any family violence — physical, emotional, financial,
  or psychological?" (The FLA s. 1 definition of family violence is
  broad; users often don't label coercive control or financial abuse as
  violence. Ask about behaviours, not labels.)
- "Do you feel safe communicating directly with the other party?"

## Interpreting the answers

### Signs negotiation or mediation may work

- Both parties can communicate civilly
- The other party has shown willingness to discuss issues
- Conflict level is moderate (under 6/10)
- No safety concerns or significant power imbalance
- Both parties have roughly realistic expectations

### Signs the court process may be necessary

- Communication has broken down completely
- The other party refuses to engage or respond
- High conflict (7+/10) with entrenched positions
- One party is making unrealistic demands and not moving
- History of broken agreements or bad-faith negotiation
- Orders are needed because agreements don't get followed

### Signs to recommend against direct negotiation

- Any family violence, past or present
- Significant power imbalance — financial, emotional, coercive control
- One party is afraid of the other
- A history of manipulation or intimidation in discussions

In these cases recommend lawyer-assisted negotiation, shuttle mediation,
or court — never direct negotiation. Where there is violence, see the
Safety section of SKILL.md first.

## Assessing the user's emotional state

Gauge where the user is, and shape the work accordingly:

- **Recently separated, in crisis** → immediate needs, stabilization,
  interim orders; defer the big strategic decisions.
- **Angry and wanting to "win"** → gently redirect to the children's
  interests, the real cost of litigation, and what courts can actually
  order. A trial "win" that costs two years and the co-parenting
  relationship is rarely a win.
- **Overwhelmed and confused** → small steps, one urgent thing at a
  time, write everything down in CASE.md.
- **Ready to move forward constructively** → good candidate for
  negotiation or mediation; help them prepare a realistic proposal.

## Record it

Summarize the assessment in CASE.md (a short table works: communication
level, preferred channel, willingness to negotiate, conflict level,
safety concerns). It calibrates every later session — and revisit it
when things change; conflict levels move in both directions over a
case's life.
