# Approaches to Resolution

Present the realistic options with honest pros and cons, tailored to the
party assessment (`party-assessment.md`). Courts themselves prefer that
parties resolve matters out of court (FLA Part 2 makes this explicit),
and in Provincial Court *early resolution registries* the preference has
teeth: parties must complete early resolution requirements (needs
assessment, and often a consensual dispute resolution attempt) before an
application can be filed — see PCFR Part 2 (e.g.
`generated/pcfr/rule_010_early_resolution_requirements_must_be_met_before_application_filed.md`)
and check whether the user's registry is one.

## Negotiation and agreement

### Direct negotiation
Parties communicate directly or through lawyers to reach agreement.
- *Pros*: lowest cost, fastest, preserves the co-parenting relationship,
  flexible outcomes courts can't order
- *Cons*: needs functional communication; unsafe with violence or power
  imbalance

### Mediation
A neutral third party facilitates agreement. Family justice counsellors
at Family Justice Centres mediate parenting and support matters free of
charge; private mediators charge but move faster.
- *Pros*: structured, neutral support, free or moderate cost, high
  resolution rates
- *Cons*: non-binding until written up and signed (then enforceable as
  an agreement); requires both parties to show up in good faith

### Collaborative process
Each party retains a collaborative lawyer; everyone commits in writing
to settling without court (the lawyers must withdraw if it fails).
- *Pros*: legal advice throughout, structured, interest-based
- *Cons*: higher cost; the withdrawal commitment raises the price of
  failure

### Parenting coordination
For implementing an existing parenting plan amid ongoing high conflict
(FLA Part 2 Division 3).
- *Pros*: fast decisions on day-to-day disputes; fewer court returns
- *Cons*: costly; limited to implementation, not major changes

**Formalizing an agreement:** a written, signed agreement on parenting
or support can be filed with the court and enforced like an order (see
FLA ss. 44, 58, 148, 163). Filing is cheap insurance — recommend it
whenever an agreement is reached.

## Court processes

### Consent order
Parties agree on terms; the court formalizes them.
- *Pros*: enforceable, quick once agreed, no contested hearing
- *Cons*: still requires drafting and filing care

### Uncontested / desk order
One party applies; the other does not oppose. (This is also how
uncontested divorces proceed in Supreme Court.)
- *Pros*: no court appearance needed
- *Cons*: only for genuinely uncontested matters

### Contested interim application
A judge decides a discrete issue before trial, usually on affidavits.
- *Pros*: binding interim relief when something can't wait
- *Cons*: adversarial, position-entrenching; affidavit evidence only

### Trial
Full hearing with oral evidence and cross-examination.
- *Pros*: thorough testing of evidence; resolves what nothing else could
- *Cons*: the most expensive, slowest, most adversarial option — months
  of preparation (see `trial-preparation.md`), and in Supreme Court the
  loser usually pays costs

## Timing considerations

### Interim orders
Temporary orders while the case proceeds.
- *Pros*: quick relief for urgent needs (where the children live,
  interim support); stabilizes the situation
- *Cons*: interim arrangements have gravity — they often become the
  status quo the final order preserves. Treat "temporary" seriously.

### Final orders
- *Pros*: certainty after full consideration
- *Cons*: slow; parenting and support remain variable on material
  change anyway (see `post-judgment.md`)

## General guidance by situation

- **Urgent safety issues** (violence, a child at risk) → protection
  order or urgent application first; resolution processes can wait.
- **Parenting disputes** → mediation first unless violence or power
  imbalance makes it unsafe.
- **Child support** → it is the child's right and the tables mostly
  decide it; try agreement using the official tables, and go to court
  mainly when income is disputed or hidden.
- **Spousal support** → wide discretion means wide settlement ranges;
  exchange full financial disclosure before negotiating, or the
  negotiation is theatre.
- **Property** → meaningful negotiation requires disclosure first;
  remember property claims belong in Supreme Court and carry costs
  exposure.
- Throughout: every escalation is one-way. It is easy to go from
  mediation to court and nearly impossible to come back, because
  litigation positions harden into identities. Escalate deliberately.
